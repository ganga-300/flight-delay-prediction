\section{Dataset and Data Quality}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Source and target}
The dataset, provided for the course, is the Kaggle \emph{Flights} table: all 336{,}776 flights
departing the three New York City airports (EWR, JFK, LGA) in 2013, with 21 columns of scheduled
and actual times, carrier, route and distance. The target is $y = 1$ if the arrival delay is at
least 15 minutes, the U.S.\ Bureau of Transportation Statistics on-time convention~\cite{bts_ontime},
and $y = 0$ otherwise. Flights without an arrival-delay value (cancelled, or departed with no
arrival record) are excluded, so the model estimates the probability of a delay given that the
flight operates and arrives. Cancellations and diversions are a separate prediction problem.

\subsection{Chronological split and sealed test set}
The data are split by date: training January--August (217{,}727 labelled flights, 26.4\%
delayed), validation September--October (55{,}628, 15.7\%) and test November--December (53{,}991,
25.8\%). Before any analysis, the November--December rows were moved out of the development data
(the full file's SHA-256 was recorded). Every notebook, feature build and training script reads a
January--October file through a loader that rejects later dates. The test rows were read once, in
the final evaluation (Section~\ref{sec:results}). No figure in this section uses test data.

\subsection{Data quality}
On the January--October development data (281{,}373 rows) there are no duplicate rows, no
duplicate flight keys and no error-level violations: all clock times are well formed, and
departure and arrival delays agree with actual minus scheduled times once midnight wrap-around is
allowed.

\textbf{Missing values} (Fig.~\ref{fig:missing}) are structured rather than random. The
departure fields are missing together for exactly the 6{,}997 cancelled flights (2.5\%); the
arrival fields are missing for 8{,}018 flights, i.e.\ the cancelled ones plus 1{,}021 that departed
without an arrival record (0.4\%). These 8{,}018 rows have no label and are dropped. The aircraft
tail number is missing for 2{,}169 flights, all of them cancelled. Because that missingness reveals
the outcome, the tail number is not used as a feature. Actual departure and arrival times are
post-departure information: departure delay alone reaches a single-feature ROC-AUC of 0.898
against the target, so these fields are excluded from all features.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{eda_missing_values.png}
\caption{Missing values by column, January--October. Only six columns have gaps, and they nest:
the departure fields are missing for cancelled flights, the arrival fields for those plus flights
with no arrival record. A random gap pattern would not nest like this.}
\label{fig:missing}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Cancellations} (Fig.~\ref{fig:cancel}) are concentrated in February (5.1\%) and early
summer, and in the regional carriers (SkyWest 11.1\%, Mesa 9.8\%, Endeavor 6.1\%). Removing them
therefore removes slightly more of the worst operating days from the labelled data, which is why
the target is defined conditional on the flight operating.

\begin{figure*}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{eda_cancellations.png}
\caption{Cancellation rate by month (January--October) and by carrier. Cancellations are not
spread evenly, so they cannot be treated as random missing labels; they are excluded and the
model predicts delay given that the flight operates.}
\label{fig:cancel}
\end{figure*}

\subsection{Exploratory analysis on the training period only}
All exploratory statistics and figures below use January--August only (217{,}727 labelled
flights), so nothing about the validation or test months influenced feature design. Rates by group
are shown with 95\% Wilson score intervals, which stay inside $[0,1]$ and remain sensible for small
groups, where the normal approximation does not.

\textbf{Target.} Arrival delay is strongly right-skewed (Fig.~\ref{fig:hist}): the median flight
arrives 4 minutes early and 56\% arrive before schedule, but the mean is $+9$ minutes because of a
long tail (9.9\% are at least an hour late; skewness 3.5). This is why we predict the BTS binary
target rather than regress minutes, which a few extreme flights would dominate. At the 15-minute
threshold 26.4\% of flights are delayed, a moderate class imbalance; we therefore use PR-AUC as
the primary metric and balanced class weights.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{eda_arrival_delay_hist.png}
\caption{Arrival delay, January--August (clipped to $[-80,300]$ min for display). The vertical line
is the 15-minute target threshold; the 26.4\% of flights to its right form the positive class.}
\label{fig:hist}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Time of day} is the strongest single pattern (Fig.~\ref{fig:hour}). The delay rate rises
almost monotonically from 10.8\% for 05:00 departures to 40.5\% at 21:00, because delays in an
aircraft's earlier legs carry forward through the day. The lower panel shows that the late
evening hours have few flights, so their intervals are wider. This motivates the scheduled
departure hour, its sine/cosine encoding (so 23:00 and 05:00 are close) and a departure-time
bucket used in carrier crosses.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{eda_delay_by_hour.png}
\caption{Delay rate by scheduled departure hour with 95\% intervals (top) and the number of
flights per hour (bottom), January--August. The dashed line is the overall rate, 26.4\%.}
\label{fig:hour}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Month and weekday} (Fig.~\ref{fig:monthweek}). Thursday is the worst day (31.0\%) and
Saturday the best (18.5\%), a stable weekly pattern that we encode as day of week, a weekend flag
and sine/cosine terms. Monthly rates vary between 22.7\% (May) and 32.2\% (July), but the pattern is
irregular, and training covers January--August only: a month or day-of-year feature would have
no training examples for September--December and could only extrapolate. Month and day-of-year
are therefore excluded; recent conditions are captured by the historical delay-rate features
instead.

\begin{figure*}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{eda_delay_by_month_weekday.png}
\caption{Delay rate by month (left) and day of week (right), January--August, with 95\%
intervals. The weekday pattern is used as a feature; the month pattern is not, because the later
months are never seen in training.}
\label{fig:monthweek}
\end{figure*}

\textbf{Hour $\times$ month} (Fig.~\ref{fig:hourmonth}). The rise through the day appears in every
month, but its height changes: in June and July evening departures exceed 50\% (July 22:00,
63.9\%), while in May the same hours stay near 30\%. The hour effect is therefore stable in shape
but scaled by current conditions, which a fixed hour feature cannot capture alone. This supports
adding recent delay rates (previous day, last 7 and 28 days) alongside the calendar features.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{eda_hour_month_heatmap.png}
\caption{Delay rate by scheduled departure hour and month, January--August (cells with fewer than
100 flights left blank). Darker cells are higher delay rates.}
\label{fig:hourmonth}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Carrier} (Fig.~\ref{fig:airline}). Carriers differ widely, from ExpressJet (35.3\%) to
American (20.3\%) and Hawaiian (14.4\%). Group sizes also differ widely, which is why intervals are
shown: SkyWest has only 7 flights (5 delayed), so its 71\% rate has a Wilson interval of
$[0.36, 0.92]$ and is not reliable evidence. This motivates the carrier categorical feature with
rare levels grouped as \emph{other} (fewer than 50 training rows), and past delay rates shrunk
towards the global rate so that small groups do not produce extreme values.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{eda_delay_by_airline.png}
\caption{Delay rate by airline, January--August, with 95\% Wilson intervals and flight counts.
The dashed line is the overall rate.}
\label{fig:airline}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Origin airport} (Fig.~\ref{fig:origin}). Newark is the most delay-prone origin (28.5\%),
then JFK (25.9\%) and LaGuardia (24.4\%). The differences are small but, with over 65{,}000 flights
per airport, well outside the intervals, so origin is kept as a feature.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{eda_delay_by_origin.png}
\caption{Delay rate by origin airport, January--August, with 95\% intervals and flight counts.}
\label{fig:origin}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Carrier $\times$ origin} (Fig.~\ref{fig:carrierorigin}). The airport effect is not the same
for every carrier: Endeavor is delayed 19.1\% of the time at EWR but 29.5\% at JFK, while
ExpressJet is worst at EWR (36.5\%). An additive model with separate carrier and origin terms cannot
represent this, so we add carrier--origin, carrier--destination, carrier--route and
carrier--departure-time crosses, which let Logistic Regression learn these interactions directly.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\columnwidth]{eda_carrier_origin_heatmap.png}
\caption{Delay rate by carrier and origin airport, January--August (cells with fewer than 500
flights left blank). The ranking of airports changes between carriers.}
\label{fig:carrierorigin}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Destination and route} (Fig.~\ref{fig:destroute}). Among the 25 busiest
destinations the rate ranges from 20.4\% (Las Vegas) to 30.5\% (Nashville), and among the 20
busiest routes from 19.0\% to 29.2\%, with several intervals clear of the overall rate. Destination
and route are therefore kept as categorical features (with rare levels grouped) and as past-only
historical delay rates. Most routes have far fewer flights than those shown, which is why the
historical rates are shrunk towards the global rate.

\begin{figure*}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.48\textwidth]{eda_delay_by_destination.png}\hfill
\includegraphics[width=0.48\textwidth]{eda_delay_by_route.png}
\caption{Delay rate for the 25 busiest destinations (left) and the 20 busiest
origin--destination routes (right), January--August, with 95\% intervals.}
\label{fig:destroute}
\end{figure*}

\textbf{Day-to-day variation} (Fig.~\ref{fig:daily}). The daily delay rate swings between 6.5\%
and 79.2\% (8 March), far more than any calendar pattern explains; bad days come in short runs
(the dataset has no weather fields, so their cause cannot be checked here). The lag-1 autocorrelation is 0.356, but lags 2 and 7 are near zero (0.041
and 0.021): yesterday's delays are informative, but persistence beyond that is weak. This is the
evidence for the 1-day historical delay rates, which at the end of day $D{-}1$ are the most recent
outcomes available, and for keeping the 7- and 28-day windows as slower background levels.

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{eda_daily_delay_rate.png}
\caption{Daily delay rate over the training period with a 7-day moving average; the dashed line
is the overall rate. Bad days cluster in short runs, so the previous day's delay rate is a useful
signal one day ahead.}
\label{fig:daily}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Summary.} Each feature group in Section~\ref{sec:features} follows from a figure above:
calendar features from Figs.~\ref{fig:hour} and~\ref{fig:monthweek}; categorical and cross
features from Figs.~\ref{fig:airline}--\ref{fig:destroute}; historical delay rates from
Figs.~\ref{fig:hourmonth} and~\ref{fig:daily}; and the exclusions (actual times, tail number,
month, day of year) from Figs.~\ref{fig:missing} and~\ref{fig:monthweek}.
